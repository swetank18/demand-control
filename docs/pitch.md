## 1. The bill, not the meter

An Indian HT commercial bill has three levers:

This building, June 2017, uncontrolled — straight from the bill engine:

| Lever | Amount | Share of bill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy charge (154,250 kWh across four ToD windows) | ₹954,407 | 72.3% |
| **Demand charge (523.3 kVA × ₹608)** | **₹318,175** | **24.1%** |
| Electricity duty | ₹47,720 | 3.6% |
| **Total** | **₹1,320,302** | |

Point at the middle row. Roughly a quarter of the bill is set by **one
thirty-minute block** — here, 12:00 on 20 June. Nobody optimises that row.

Note where that peak sits: midday, in the *normal* tariff window, not in the
18:00–22:00 peak window. Time-of-day arbitrage would not have touched it.

## 2. One bad Tuesday

One extra hour of load on one Tuesday afternoon:

- costs **₹1,270** in energy
- costs **₹119,200** in demand charge
- a factor of **94**

That is a test, not a slide claim: `tests/test_bill.py::test_one_bad_tuesday_sets_the_month`.

Fifteen seconds. Then move on.

## 3. What everyone builds

Load forecast → dashboard → "we predict your consumption with 3% MAPE." Say it
plainly; it earns the credibility for slide 5.

An accurate forecast is not a control system, and the demand charge is not a
prediction problem. It is a risk problem: you are exposed to the *maximum*, and
the maximum is set by the tail of the forecast, not its centre.

## 4. What we built

The architecture diagram from the README. Two rules to say out loud:

- **No language model touches a money decision.** The optimiser is a
  deterministic MILP. The tariff parser is regex plus a human confirmation step
  that refuses to emit an unreviewed tariff.
- **The bill engine is the single source of truth**, matched to the rupee
  against a hand computation for a full week.

## 5. Why not reinforcement learning

Across CityLearn 2021, 2022 and 2023 at NeurIPS, **none** of the top-performing
teams used RL for scheduling. Winners used classical optimisation and heuristics,
for want of a simulation environment rich enough to train on. A hackathon has
less of that than the competition did.

This reads as judgment, not as a gap. Cite it and move on.

## 6. Calibration is the safety property

The whole system rests on one substitution:

```
base_q95[t] + controllable[t] - solar_q05[t]  <=  D_target
```

If q95 is not really a 95th percentile, that guarantee is theatre. So it is
measured, on held-out June data the model never saw:

| Building | 90% interval coverage | P(y ≤ q95) | worst horizon |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Office (Gaylord) | 0.890 | 0.939 | 0.888 |
| Assembly (Dixie) | 0.920 | 0.953 | 0.916 |
| Education (Etta) | 0.926 | 0.960 | 0.922 |
| Public (Denny) | 0.896 | 0.939 | 0.893 |

Show the reliability diagram. Then the honest detail, and it is the strongest
thing on this slide: split conformal calibrated on April–May **under-covers June
at 83.2%** — below our own acceptance floor of 85%. June is hotter and the load
is bigger; a textbook distribution shift. Adaptive conformal closes the loop
online, updating each quantile against its realised exceedance rate, and restores
**89.0%**, paying 6.4 kW of extra interval width (32.2 → 38.6 kW) for it.

So the guarantee was not free and it was not automatic. Without that layer the
forecaster fails its own test, and every q95 the controller relies on would have
been an overstatement.

That is the slide that separates this from a dashboard.

## 8. The results table

June 2017, one real building, one real tariff, identical weather and base load
for every row.

**Normal month** (demand ceiling 467 kW):

| Controller | Bill ₹ | Peak kVA | Breaches | Comfort % | % of oracle savings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No control | 1,320,302 | 523.3 | 11 | 0.00 | 0.0 |
| Rule based schedule | 1,287,528 | 528.2 | 31 | 0.00 | 15.5 |
| MPC on mean forecast | 1,157,010 | 494.3 | 2 | 0.62 | 77.4 |
| **Ours: quantile + chance constrained** | **1,148,853** | **480.7** | **0** | 0.56 | **81.2** |
| MPC on perfect forecast (16 h) | 1,143,366 | 472.6 | 0 | 0.66 | 83.8 |
| Perfect foresight oracle (whole month) | 1,109,256 | 483.3 | 0 | 0.73 | 100.0 |

**Heatwave** (+6 °C, base load +25%, three days) — the row that matters:

| Controller | Bill ₹ | Peak kVA | Breaches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MPC on mean forecast | 1,191,991 | 502.6 | **4** |
| **Ours** | **1,180,080** | **482.6** | **0** |
| MPC on perfect forecast | 1,179,468 | 482.6 | 0 |

Under the heatwave, ours lands within ₹612 of a controller with a **perfect
forecast**, at the same comfort cost, while the mean-forecast controller breaches
the ceiling four times.

The oracle row is a genuine whole-month optimum, not a rolling controller with
good forecasts — so "81% of oracle savings" means 81% of what was actually on the
table.

## 9. What is real and what is not

Read from `docs/limitations.md`, written before the results existed. The short
version: the tariff, bill engine, meter data, calibration and physics are real;
the buildings are American, the source data is hourly upsampled to 15 minutes,
the split of the meter into base load and HVAC is inferred, and the PV array is a
design scenario.

End on portability: swap the tariff object, rerun. `tariff/orders/msedcl_2026.json`
is a second state, and `tests/test_bill.py` asserts that the same code produces a
different answer — in Maharashtra midday is the *cheap* window, which inverts the
shifting strategy.

## Three findings worth volunteering before a judge finds them

1. **The tariff-aware rule-based baseline makes the bill worse on peak.** It
   pre-cools ahead of the peak window and its own pre-cooling sets a higher
   monthly demand (528.2 kVA vs 523.3 for doing nothing). Tariff-awareness
   without risk-awareness is actively harmful.
2. **The achievable shave is 6%, and it is bounded by physics, not by the
   algorithm.** With a 4 K comfort band and ISO 13790 "medium" thermal mass,
   there is only so much cold you can bank. The oracle only reaches 483 kVA
   itself. We report the gap to the oracle rather than to a strawman for exactly
   this reason.
3. **Standard measurement and verification would under-report our own saving by
   3×.** See `results/mv_baseline_length.json`: with a 2-month baseline, M&V
   reports ₹45,957 against a true ₹171,448 — and its 95% band excludes the truth.
   Only at twelve months of baseline does the band cover it. We would rather say
   this than have a judge discover it.
