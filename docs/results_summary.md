### The ablation — the experiment the whole round turns on

Same optimiser, same month, same tariff, physics, comfort budget, PV and seed.
The only thing that changes is the file the base-load quantiles are read from
(`results/ablation.md`).

| Forecaster feeding the optimiser | Pinball | Breaches | Peak kVA | Bill ₹ | Usable headroom kW |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Static margin (no forecast) | 20.307 | 11 | 503.4 | 1,162,491 | 240.9 |
| Persistence | 27.582 | 87 | 556.8 | 1,203,766 | 342.9 |
| Seasonal naive | 6.213 | 0 | 482.8 | 1,150,421 | 339.2 |
| Climatology | 7.314 | 1 | 494.8 | 1,157,695 | 328.8 |
| Linear quantile | 10.758 | 0 | 490.5 | 1,155,519 | 314.1 |
| **LightGBM quantile (ours)** | **2.397** | **0** | **481.9** | **1,149,605** | **354.1** |
| Neural quantile | 2.435 | 0 | 477.5 | 1,146,700 | 359.7 |
| Perfect foresight | 0.000 | 0 | 480.9 | 1,148,785 | 376.0 |

Replacing the forecaster with a constant costs **+11 breaches, ₹12,885 and
113 kW of usable headroom**. Two rows deserve to be read out rather than skipped:

- **Seasonal naive already reaches zero breaches** on this calm month. Our
  advantage over it here is 15 kW of headroom, a lower peak, ₹816 and a pinball
  loss 2.6× better. Stated carefully: on a calm month against a building with a
  timetable, last week's shape is a genuinely good forecast, and in the
  cold-start study below it beats our *transferred* model. It is the right
  day-one reference at a new site. What it lacks is margin when the month stops
  being calm.
- **Persistence claims the second-most headroom in the table and breaches 87
  times.** Headroom without calibration is an overdraft, not a benefit, and that
  row is why coverage rather than accuracy is the operative metric.

### Under a heatwave, and it does not flatter us

`eval/ablation.py --stress none heatwave`. Same eight forecasters, same fixed
optimiser, +6 °C and +25% base load for three days.

| Forecaster feeding the optimiser | Breaches | Peak kVA | Bill ₹ | Interval width kW |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Static margin (no forecast) | 15 | 506.2 | 1,194,198 | 223.4 |
| Persistence | 104 | 556.8 | 1,233,458 | 314.7 |
| **Seasonal naive** | **0** | 487.4 | 1,183,179 | 72.2 |
| Climatology | 3 | 497.1 | 1,189,003 | 86.5 |
| Linear quantile | 3 | 501.6 | 1,192,089 | 132.5 |
| **LightGBM quantile (ours)** | **2** | 501.6 | 1,191,414 | 39.6 |
| Neural quantile | 2 | 502.1 | 1,191,609 | 33.5 |
| Perfect foresight | 2 | 497.2 | 1,188,476 | 0.0 |

**Read this one carefully, because it is against us.** Seasonal naive takes zero
breaches where we take two — and so does a *perfect* forecast of the unstressed
month. The mechanism is visible in the last column: under a shock that is in
nobody's inputs, what protects the ceiling is interval **width**, and seasonal
naive's intervals are 1.8× wider than ours. Sharpness is what buys headroom on
an ordinary month and it is what costs you on a day the model could not have
known about.

Two things follow, and we would rather say both than have them found:

1. **This experiment measures margin, not adaptation.** The forecast tensors are
   computed offline on the unstressed month, so no forecaster here can react to
   the heatwave at all. The adaptive conformal layer's actual response to a
   regime shift is measured separately, and it is the April→June result: split
   conformal alone falls to 0.832 coverage, adaptive restores 0.890 by widening
   online. That is the mechanism that would fire on a real heatwave and it is
   switched off by construction in this table.
2. **The honest summary of the whole ablation is narrower than "our model
   wins".** It is: remove the forecast entirely and you lose 11 breaches and
   113 kW of headroom; keep a forecast and the differences between good ones are
   real but modest on a calm month, and can invert under an unmodelled shock.

### Does the ablation hold on another building? Partly, and we say which part

Same eight forecasters, same optimiser, same protocol, on the event-driven
assembly hall — the hardest building in the set to forecast (median MAPE 0.69).
Its ceiling, 213 kW, was found by the same bisection.

| Building | Ceiling | Our pinball | Breaches: ours / no model / persistence | Headroom gained | Bill vs no model |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Office tower (Gaylord) | 467 kW | 2.397 | 0 / 11 / 87 | +113.2 kW | ₹12,885 better |
| Assembly hall (Dixie) | 213 kW | 4.927 | 1 / 0 / 255 | +42.9 kW | ₹1,041 **worse** |

**The office result does not reproduce on the hall, and that is the finding.**
Two structural reasons, neither of them luck. The demand ceiling is found by
bisecting with *our* forecaster, so we sit at the tightest point it can hold and
any more conservative controller clears it by scheduling less. And on a load this
erratic, a wide constant margin is a defensible forecast — the same mechanism as
the heatwave row above.

What does survive on both buildings: the forecast hands back 113 kW and 43 kW of
usable capacity against the no-model constant, and persistence is catastrophic on
both (87 and 255 breaches). The claim the evidence supports is therefore narrower
than "our model always wins": **a forecast buys capacity, and a badly calibrated
one destroys the ceiling. Whether a good forecast also buys rupees depends on how
predictable the building is.**

### The exchange rate between forecast quality and money

`results/model_frontier.json`, figure `06_model_frontier.png`. Fitted across the
family of forecasters: **₹2,228 per unit of pinball loss per month** (R² 0.95)
and **3.5 ceiling breaches per unit** (R² 0.89). Calibration error against
breaches is monotonic (Spearman ρ = 0.85, p = 0.03).

Headroom against pinball is **not** monotonic, and that is reported as the
finding it is: persistence buys headroom it cannot pay for. The like-for-like
comparison is the static-margin study below, which matches breach counts first.

### Why a forecast at all, in kilowatts

A forecast-free static derating, swept from p50 to the training maximum, with
everything else held fixed. The setting that is as safe as we are (zero breaches)
leaves **198.1 kW** usable. We leave **354.1 kW**. The forecast recovers
**156 kW, +79% of usable capacity at equal safety**.

Worth volunteering: the sweep is not monotonic in breaches (p80 breaches five
times, p90 twelve). A margin that is too tight defers load into a rebound peak,
so "more conservative" does not reliably mean "safer" — which is itself an
argument against fixed margins.

### Does it work on a building it has never seen?

`Fox_public_Denny` held out of training entirely, with 14 days of its own history
to set scale (109 kW) and fill lags.

| Model | Pinball | Cov 90% | q95 hit | MAE kW |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trained on this building (the ceiling) | 2.576 | 0.897 | 0.939 | 8.11 |
| Cold start — never saw this building | 3.546 | 0.891 | 0.940 | 12.70 |
| Seasonal naive | 3.037 | 0.878 | 0.939 | 9.40 |

Cold start costs 38% of pinball and **loses to seasonal naive on this building**.
What survives the transfer is the property the controller depends on: coverage
0.891 against a nominal 0.90 on a building the model has never seen. So the
honest deployment story is seasonal naive for the first fortnight, the pooled
model's interval from day one, and a site-specific model once there is a season
of data.

### Interpretability

- **SHAP** on the q95 booster: `hod_cos`, `dow_sin`, `solar_elev`, `cdd_fut`,
  `rmean_672`, `t_out_fut`.
- **Feature-group ablation** (drop, retrain, rescore on validation): calendar
  **+177%** pinball, weather forecast **+34%**, recent lags **−1.3%**, rolling
  statistics **−6.8%**.
  SHAP says what the model used; the retrain says what it needed, and sixteen
  hours ahead the recent lags carry almost nothing. That is the same fact that
  sinks persistence in the ablation.
  **Caveat, stated where the number is:** weather at the target time is the
  recorded observation — a perfect weather forecast — so the +34% is an upper
  bound on what the weather feed is worth. `--weather-noise-c` degrades it, and
  `docs/limitations.md` flagged the assumption before any of this ran.
- **The worst evening**: 19 June 14:30, actual 290 kW against a q95 of 264 kW, a
  26 kW exceedance, day q95 hit rate 0.596. No breach followed — there were
  203 kW of headroom under the target at that moment.

| Technical | peak 41.4 kVA lower (7.9%), breaches 11 → 0, **99.5%** of the achievable saving captured |

The financial split is worth stating before anyone asks: most of the rupees on
this building are energy, from the controller using its full comfort band where
the baseline thermostat holds a fixed setpoint. Any competent scheduler gets that
part. The demand-charge line is smaller and it is the only one that needs the
forecast — it is the line the ablation moves.

## Forecast calibration (held-out June 2017)

| Building | cov90 | P(y ≤ q95) | worst-horizon cov90 | MAE (kW) | mean interval (kW) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fox_office_Gaylord | 0.890 | 0.939 | 0.888 | 7.1 | 38.6 |
| Fox_assembly_Dixie | 0.920 | 0.953 | 0.916 | 15.5 | 72.4 |
| Fox_education_Etta | 0.926 | 0.960 | 0.922 | 7.9 | 53.3 |
| Fox_public_Denny | 0.896 | 0.939 | 0.893 | 8.3 | 36.2 |

Horizon is 64 steps (16 hours), long enough for a controller at midnight to see
the next afternoon's peak — the precondition for pre-cooling into thermal mass.
An 8-hour horizon reaches 08:00 and the peak is at 14:00.

Split conformal alone under-covers June at **0.832**, below the 0.85 acceptance
floor, because it was calibrated on April–May and June is hotter: a genuine
distribution shift. Adaptive conformal restores **0.890**, widening the mean
interval from 32.2 to 38.6 kW. Reproduce the failing case with
`forecast/train.py --buildings Fox_office_Gaylord --no-adaptive`.

Note that `Fox_assembly_Dixie` has a median-forecast MAPE of 0.69 — it is an
event-driven hall and genuinely unpredictable — yet its intervals still cover at
0.920. The system does not need to predict that building well; it needs to know
how badly it predicts it, and it does.

## Solver performance

Mean MILP solve 4–8 ms, p95 ~12 ms, 64-step horizon at 15-minute resolution,
HiGHS via `scipy.optimize.milp`. Relaxing the water-heater binaries beyond the
first 8 steps cut mean solve time from 51 ms to 4.4 ms with no material change to
the answer (bill within 0.3%, identical breach count).

## The comfort/savings frontier

The operator picks the ceiling; the system reports the lowest demand it can then
commit to. `results/frontier.json`, occupied band floor fixed at 22 °C:

| Comfort ceiling | Band | Demand target | Shave | Saving vs no control | Comfort violated |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 24 °C | 2 K | 463 kW | 6.9% | ₹124,225 | 0.87% |
| 25 °C | 3 K | 463 kW | 6.9% | ₹149,479 | 0.62% |
| 26 °C | 4 K | 463 kW | 6.9% | ₹171,589 | 0.59% |
| 27 °C | 5 K | 446 kW | 10.3% | ₹198,262 | 0.49% |
| 28 °C | 6 K | 446 kW | 10.3% | ₹220,311 | 0.21% |

Two things to note. The saving nearly doubles across the range, so comfort is the
real lever and it belongs to the operator, not to us. And violations *fall* as
the band widens — a wider band is not a promise to be less comfortable, it is
more room to manoeuvre, so the controller is cornered less often.

The frontier search ran a coarser bisection (4 iterations) than the headline
target search (7), and found 463 kW holdable at the 26 °C ceiling where the
headline table uses 467 kW. The table is therefore slightly conservative.
