\begin{abstract}
Demand charges in Indian commercial tariffs are set by a single worst
thirty-minute block in a month, which makes defending a demand ceiling a risk
problem rather than a prediction problem. We built a tariff-native controller
for this and then, rather than reporting it on the building it was developed on,
subjected it to a cross-country benchmark: one unchanged protocol over
twenty-six electricity supplies in seven countries --- eighteen individual
buildings across eight usage classes from the Building Data Genome Project 2,
and six national or city demand series including real fifteen-minute data from
the Delhi State Load Despatch Centre and European transmission data from
ENTSO-E. India is the focal case throughout, and it is simultaneously the most
favourable and the most difficult series in the study.

Favourable, because India sits at one extreme of every axis that makes the
method worth deploying: the highest cooling-degree-day share in the benchmark
(0.89), the only strongly positive load--temperature correlation ($+0.59$,
against $-0.64$ for France), and among the peakiest load profiles. Difficult,
because it is last on both axes that decide whether the method can be trusted:
the lowest forecast skill over a seasonal-naive baseline ($+0.097$, against
$+0.713$ for Germany) and the worst probabilistic calibration in the study
(0.762 coverage of a nominal 90\% interval).

We claim no new algorithm. Every component --- quantile gradient boosting,
conformalized and adaptive conformal prediction, chance-constrained and
scenario-based model predictive control --- is established. The contribution is
an instrument in which each component can be pinned while exactly one is
swapped, and four measurements it makes possible. First, a per-step exceedance
rate of $0.062$ corresponds to a $0.405$ probability of breaching somewhere in a
sixteen-hour planning window, while the independence bound gives $0.983$: the
common formulation is under-protected roughly sevenfold and the textbook
correction is $2.4\times$ too loose. Second, a scenario controller over sampled
joint trajectories converts that risk level into an operator-set dial, tracking
requested $\varepsilon$ with rank correlation $0.957$ for \rupee{3,339} on the
month. Third, conformal coverage degrades with aggregation level and fails worst
on Indian demand. Fourth, forecast skill is uncorrelated with climate while
available flexibility tracks it strongly, so deployment screening should ask how
much load is weather-driven rather than how predictable it is. We close by
stating precisely what India must fix, and three of our results argue against
the method and are reported unchanged.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An Indian commercial electricity bill has three levers: a time-of-day energy
charge, a demand charge levied on the highest 30-minute average demand in the
month, and power-factor penalties. The second dominates the risk. It is set by
one block out of roughly 1{,}440 in a month, so a single bad afternoon prices
all thirty days. A controller that reduces average consumption but occasionally
overshoots can cost more than one that does nothing.

The standard applied response is a chance constraint: replace the forecast mean
in the capacity constraint with a high quantile, typically the 95th, and treat
the result as a 95\% guarantee. Formally, with $b_t$ the uncontrollable base
load, $u_t$ the controllable allocation, $s_t$ solar generation and $D$ the
ceiling,
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat b^{\,q95}_t + u_t - \hat s^{\,q05}_t \;\le\; D
  \qquad \text{for each } t \text{ in the horizon.}
  \label{eq:marginal}
\end{equation}
%
Equation~\eqref{eq:marginal} is a set of \emph{marginal} statements. Each row
says that at that timestep, considered alone, the realisation exceeds the bound
about 5\% of the time. The event that costs money is different: it is
%
\begin{equation}
  \Pr\Big[\max_{t \in \mathcal{W}} \big(b_t + u_t - s_t\big) > D\Big],
  \label{eq:joint}
\end{equation}
%
a statement about a maximum over a window $\mathcal{W}$. That
\eqref{eq:marginal} does not imply \eqref{eq:joint} is elementary and
well known; joint chance constraints, their intractability, and the standard
routes around them --- Boole/Bonferroni risk allocation and the scenario
approach \citep{calafiore2006scenario} --- are textbook material.

\paragraph{What is not established} is how large the gap is on real building
load, and how badly the conservative bound overshoots it. Both ends matter
practically: the first is how much a deployed controller is under-protected, and
the second is how much headroom a careful designer gives away for nothing. We
measure both.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A three-way bracket on horizon risk for a demand ceiling on held-out
        meter data (Section~\ref{sec:horizon}): claimed $0.05$, realised
        $0.405$, Boole bound $0.983$ at a 16-hour horizon.
  \item Evidence that conformal coverage degrades with aggregation level, from
        one protocol run over 24 series in seven countries, and fails worst on
        real Indian demand (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).
  \item A null result separating forecast skill from available flexibility as
        independent deployment axes (Section~\ref{sec:axes}).
  \item A closed-loop demonstration that the horizon risk level can be made a
        settable parameter rather than an emergent one, with its exactness and
        resolution limits measured (Section~\ref{sec:horizon}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Where the novelty is, and where it is not.} We state this
precisely, because a novelty claim that cannot survive a literature search is
worth less than no claim. \emph{Not novel:} chance-constrained model predictive
control for buildings; joint chance constraints and their standard treatments,
including Boole/Bonferroni risk allocation and the scenario approach
\citep{calafiore2006scenario}; quantile gradient boosting
\citep{ke2017lightgbm}; conformalized quantile regression \citep{romano2019cqr}
and adaptive conformal inference \citep{gibbs2021adaptive}; decision-focused
learning \citep{elmachtoub2022spo,donti2017task}; and cross-building transfer
learning, which is an active area operating on this very corpus
\citep{miller2020bdg2}. We invented none of it and do not claim to.

\emph{Novel:} the instrument, and what it measures. We are not aware of prior
work that (i) brackets the marginal-versus-horizon risk gap for a demand ceiling
on real meter data with \emph{both} ends reported, so that the common
formulation and the textbook correction are shown to be wrong in opposite
directions by measured factors; (ii) reports that distribution-free coverage
degrades systematically with aggregation level, holding at building scale and
failing at system scale; (iii) separates forecast skill from available
flexibility as empirically independent deployment criteria, with the expected
relationship reported as a null; or (iv) benchmarks a tariff-native demand
controller across seven countries with India as the focal case and the Indian
result reported as the weakest one. The novelty is empirical and integrative
rather than algorithmic. That is a narrower claim than a new method, and it is
one that holds.

\section{The instrument}

Six components, each independently swappable, with the bill engine as the single
source of truth for every reported figure:
%
\[
\text{tariff} \rightarrow \text{quantile forecast} \rightarrow
\text{chance-constrained MILP} \rightarrow \text{RC simulator}
\rightarrow \text{bill engine}.
\]
%
The forecaster is a quantile LightGBM at five levels over a 64-step
(16-hour) horizon at 15-minute resolution, wrapped in split and adaptive
conformal calibration. The controller is a deterministic MILP solved with
HiGHS; no language model touches a money decision. The evaluation discipline is
that a number reaches a table only if a script produced it, and a single
entry point regenerates every figure from a clean checkout.

This matters for the measurements because each one requires pinning everything
and moving one thing. Section~\ref{sec:horizon} pins the plant and varies only
the risk formulation; Section~\ref{sec:calibration} pins the protocol and varies
only the series; Section~\ref{sec:axes} pins the model and varies the site.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

Four public sources, all fetched by script rather than vendored, and named in
full because the benchmark's credibility rests on them.

\paragraph{Building Data Genome Project 2} \citep{miller2020bdg2}. 1{,}578
metered non-residential buildings across nineteen sites, hourly electricity with
matched weather, two years. It is the only harmonised multi-country building
corpus in existence, which is why it carries the controlled comparison: the same
physical quantity, the same metering convention, the same period. It contains
\emph{no Indian buildings}, and this is the single most consequential gap in the
study --- discussed in Section~\ref{sec:india-fix}.

\paragraph{Delhi State Load Despatch Centre.} Fifteen-minute metered demand for
the city of Delhi, disaggregated across five distribution companies (BRPL, BYPL,
NDPL, NDMC and MES) plus the total, two complete years at full coverage. This is
the only series in the entire study at \emph{native} fifteen-minute resolution
--- the cadence the controller actually runs at and the cadence on which Indian
demand charges are assessed. Every other series is interpolated up from hourly.

\paragraph{ENTSO-E transmission data, via Open Power System Data.} Hourly
national load for the United Kingdom, Ireland, Germany, France and Spain, drawn
from the European transmission system operators' transparency platform.

\paragraph{ERA5 reanalysis, via the Open-Meteo archive.} Hourly temperature, dew
point and cloud cover at each location, used identically for every series.

\paragraph{Windows.} The building tier and the European national tier share
2016--2017. The Delhi archive covers 2011--2012, so the same protocol is shifted
back five years with its shape preserved, leaving June as the test month in every
row. Absolute losses are therefore not comparable across the two groups, which is
why the headline metric throughout is skill against each series' own
seasonal-naive baseline: a ratio computed inside one series' own data and its own
period.

\section{The horizon gap}
\label{sec:horizon}

\input{tables/horizon}

Table~\ref{tab:horizon} reports, for a 13{,}759\,m$^2$ office on held-out June
2017 data over 5{,}793 forecast origins, the per-step exceedance rate of the
ceiling alongside the probability that the ceiling is breached at least once
within a window of $H$ steps.

Three readings of the last row are simultaneously true and together are the
result. The per-step quantile is \emph{well calibrated}: $0.062$ against a
nominal $0.05$, at every horizon. The forecaster is not broken and marginal
calibration is exactly what it promised. The probability of breaching somewhere
in the 16-hour window is $0.405$, roughly sevenfold the number
\eqref{eq:marginal} appears to promise. And the independence bound
$1-(1-\alpha)^H = 0.983$ is $2.4\times$ the realised value, because building
load is heavily autocorrelated --- lag-1 correlation $0.871$, decaying to
$0.086$ by lag 32 --- and Boole assumes the worst case over dependence
structures.

The practical consequence runs in both directions. A controller using
\eqref{eq:marginal} is materially under-protected. A controller using the
textbook bound is over-protected by more than a factor of two and pays for it
in usable headroom, which is the quantity the entire economic argument rests on.

\paragraph{Restoring the guarantee.} We fit a Student-$t$ copula over the
horizon --- $\nu=7$, selected on the validation block --- and sample joint
trajectories, which a scenario MILP then constrains with a CVaR surrogate on the
probability mass permitted to violate. The copula reproduces the calibrated
marginals to within 4.6\% of mean interval width at 4{,}000 paths per origin, so
it adds dependence and disturbs nothing upstream, and it predicts horizon
exceedance far better than either end of the bracket: $0.497$ against a realised
$0.405$ at $H=64$, where the marginal formulation implies $0.05$ and Boole
implies $0.983$.

\input{tables/acceptance}

Table~\ref{tab:acceptance} is a closed-loop run over a real billing month at two
demand targets and four risk levels. The acceptance metric is violation of the
ceiling \emph{the optimiser itself committed to at each origin}, because that is
the event $\varepsilon$ is a statement about. The operator's monthly demand
target is a business metric the chance constraint never promised: the committed
peak is a decision variable, and an optimiser short of capacity may lift it,
pay the demand charge, and honour its constraint exactly. Grading a chance
constraint against a number it never promised is the usual way this test is made
meaningless.

The result is that $\varepsilon$ becomes a dial. Realised commit violation tracks
the requested level with rank correlation $0.957$ across the sweep, mean
absolute gap $0.057$, and lands conservative at five of eight levels. The
formulation errs safe by construction: the CVaR surrogate admits a strict subset
of the chance-constrained feasible set, so it defends the committed ceiling
harder than asked.

\paragraph{What it does not do.} It is not exact. At the tightest setting,
$\varepsilon=0.05$ delivers $0.097$ at the tight target, nearly twice the
requested risk and on the unsafe side. There is also a hard resolution floor at
$1/S = 0.025$: with $S$ scenarios the finest expressible risk level is one
scenario's worth of probability mass, below which the CVaR surrogate collapses
onto the sample maximum and successive $\varepsilon$ cease to be
distinguishable. That is a property of sample average approximation rather than
a tuning artefact, and a finer dial costs scenarios and solve time.

The honest summary is not that the guarantee is restored exactly. It is that a
risk level which was previously an \emph{emergent property of horizon length} ---
unstated, unsettable, and equal to $0.106$ at the tight target under the
incumbent marginal controller --- is now a parameter with a monotone and
conservative response, at a cost of \rupee{3,339} on the month.

\section{Calibration degrades with aggregation}
\label{sec:calibration}

One protocol --- 15 months train, 2 months validate, 1 month test, temporal
throughout, conformal calibration on the validation block --- run unchanged over
24 series. Selection is by a rule stated in advance and applied uniformly:
no district chilled-water meter, $>$97\% coverage, median load 40--800\,kW,
positive load--temperature correlation; ties within a (site, usage) cell broken
by highest $p99/\text{median}$.

Table~\ref{tab:calibration} is the consistent finding. A nominal 90\% interval
holds at building level (mean $0.901$; one row of eighteen below $0.85$) and
fails at system level (mean $0.829$; five of six below $0.85$). Delhi is the
worst row in the study at $0.762$.

This is consequential rather than curious, because the controller's $q95$ is
read from the same object. Too narrow an interval yields too narrow a demand
ceiling, and the safety property degrades precisely on the load the project
targets. The mechanism is the exchangeability requirement of split conformal
\citep{vovk2005algorithmic,romano2019cqr}: a Delhi May is not exchangeable with
a Delhi June, because the monsoon arrives between them. Our own walk-forward
audit finds the same failure at smaller amplitude on the Phoenix office,
where monthly
coverage of a nominal 90\% interval ranges from $0.45$ to $0.94$. Whether the
adaptive layer \citep{gibbs2021adaptive} restores the level at system scale is
the obvious next experiment and is stated as one.

\paragraph{Selection is regional.} The rule's final clause --- positive
load--temperature correlation --- is a sanity check in Phoenix, where every
candidate is cooling-dominated, and a site-destroying filter in Minneapolis or
Dublin, where buildings consume electricity when it is cold. Seventy-seven
buildings across nine sites pass every clause but that one. Separately, the two
hottest sites in the corpus, the closest available analogues to an Indian metro
climate, yield no admissible building at all for want of coverage in the split
window. Both facts were discovered by applying the rule, not anticipated when
writing it.

\section{India in comparison}
\label{sec:india}

\input{tables/india}

Table~\ref{tab:india} is the comparison the benchmark exists to make, and it
resolves into a single sentence: \textbf{India is the place this method is most
worth deploying and the place it is currently least trustworthy.}

\paragraph{Why India is the favourable case.} Delhi has the highest
cooling-degree-day share in the benchmark at $0.89$, and it is the only series
whose demand rises strongly with temperature ($+0.59$). France is its mirror at
$-0.64$: French demand rises when it gets \emph{cold}, because the building stock
is electrically heated. That distinction is the whole economic argument. A
controller that buys down a cooling peak inside a demand-charge tariff has
something to work with in the first regime and almost nothing in the second.
Delhi is also among the peakiest profiles in the set ($p99/$median $1.61$),
which is precisely the shape a demand charge punishes.

\paragraph{Why India is the difficult case.} On both axes that determine whether
the controller can be relied upon, India is last. Forecast skill over a
seasonal-naive baseline is $+0.097$, against $+0.713$ for Germany, $+0.636$ for
France and $+0.619$ for Spain --- a model that needs no training at all is nearly
as good on Delhi as ours. And coverage of the nominal 90\% interval is $0.762$,
the worst figure anywhere in the study, against $0.911$ for Ireland.

The second of these is the serious one. The controller's $q95$ is read from the
same object as that interval, so an interval that is too narrow yields a demand
ceiling that is too narrow, and the safety property is weakest on exactly the
load the project targets.

\section{What India must fix}
\label{sec:india-fix}

Stated as work rather than as caveats, in descending order of how much they cost.

\paragraph{1. There is no Indian building-level load dataset.} This is a national
research-infrastructure gap and it bounds everything else. The Building Data
Genome Project 2 harmonises 1{,}578 buildings across three countries and contains
no Indian building. We were therefore forced to test the \emph{controller} on
American buildings under an Indian tariff and the \emph{forecaster} on Indian
city demand, and no single row of this study is both Indian and building-level.
Relabelling an American building as Indian would have been worse than the gap,
so the gap is reported. Until an Indian equivalent exists --- submetered,
multi-site, multi-year, openly licensed --- no one can honestly claim a
building-level demand-control result for India, ourselves included.

\paragraph{2. Calibration collapses across the monsoon.} Coverage of $0.762$ is
not a tuning failure, it is an assumption failure. Split conformal prediction
requires the calibration and test blocks to be exchangeable
\citep{vovk2005algorithmic,romano2019cqr}, and a Delhi May is not exchangeable
with a Delhi June: the monsoon arrives between them and changes the relationship
between temperature and load. The remedy is the adaptive layer
\citep{gibbs2021adaptive}, monsoon-aware calibration windows, or explicit regime
detection. We have the method; we have not yet run it at this tier, and we
decline to claim the result before we have.

\paragraph{3. Indian load carries calendar structure the model does not encode.}
A large share of Indian public holidays are lunisolar --- Diwali, Holi, Eid ---
and move against the Gregorian calendar, so they cannot be expressed as the
fixed date list that load-forecasting pipelines conventionally use, including
ours until this study forced the change. Beyond holidays, Indian demand carries
load-shedding events, agricultural pumping schedules and festival periods that
have no counterpart in the European series and no representation in our feature
set. The low skill figure of $+0.097$ should be read partly as a statement about
missing features rather than solely about the data.

\paragraph{4. The Indian series is a decade old.} The Delhi archive covers
2011--2012. India's generation mix, air-conditioning penetration and peak demand
have all moved substantially since. The load \emph{shape} conclusions are the
durable ones; the levels are not, and nothing in this paper should be read as a
statement about the present Indian grid.

\paragraph{What this does not undermine.} The favourable half of
Table~\ref{tab:india} rests on physical structure --- cooling share, the sign of
the load--temperature relationship, peakiness --- which is the part least likely
to have inverted since 2012. Indian demand has not stopped rising with
temperature.

\section{Skill and flexibility are independent axes}
\label{sec:axes}

\input{tables/correlations}

We expected forecast skill --- measured with pinball loss, a strictly proper
scoring rule \citep{gneiting2007strictly}, against each series' own
seasonal-naive baseline, a within-series ratio comparable across differing units and periods
--- to increase with cooling load, on the reasoning that weather-driven demand
is the learnable part. Table~\ref{tab:correlations} reports the opposite: a null.
Every correlation against skill is indistinguishable from zero and every
leave-one-out range straddles or nearly straddles the sign line, which at
$n=18$ is what a null result looks like.

The controllable fraction, by contrast, tracks climate strongly ($r=+0.489$),
running from 37.6\% of the meter in Phoenix to 0.8\% in London.

The deployment consequence is a screening rule. Do not ask whether a site's load
is predictable; ask how much of it is weather-driven. A Dublin office is highly
predictable (skill $+0.513$) with roughly 3\% controllable load, and there is
nothing to sell there. Conflating the axes inverts the decision.

For the target market this is favourable: Delhi demand correlates $+0.59$ with
outdoor temperature against $-0.64$ for France, placing India at the useful end
of the axis that matters.

\section{Results that argue against the method}

Three rows are reported unchanged. The London office returns skill
$-0.463$: worse than a baseline requiring no training. Delhi returns
$+0.097$ with the study's worst calibration. Public services in Washington DC
returns $+0.052$. All three sit at 1--3\% HVAC share, where a weather-aware
model has little to add over ``last week, same time'' --- a coherent and
testable explanation rather than an excuse.

Two defects in our own pipeline were exposed by running it outside its home
site. A hard-coded United States federal holiday calendar would have inverted
the holiday flag on two of the largest annual load anomalies for every European
building; holidays are now resolved per country, and India is the case that
forces it, its calendar being substantially lunisolar and therefore not
expressible as a fixed list. And a data-preparation stage overwrote its own
manifest, silently discarding every site but the last, which single-site work
had never exposed.

\section{Limitations}

The system tier is a forecasting benchmark, not a control benchmark: there is
no thermal mass to shift at grid level and no savings claim is made from it. The
Delhi window (2011--12) does not overlap the European one (2016--17), which
motivates reporting within-series skill rather than absolute loss but does not
eliminate the concern. Capital-city temperature proxies national load-weighted
temperature, acceptable for Great Britain and Ireland and rough for Germany,
France and Spain. Nothing here claims that model \emph{weights} transfer across
countries --- our own cold-start study finds the model losing to seasonal naive
on an unseen building at the same site --- only that the architecture and
protocol do. The demographic arm is a single city and cannot separate
demographic from local building stock. Delhi is one Indian city, and is real
Indian data rather than a claim about India.

\section{Conclusion}

We built an instrument rather than an algorithm, and ran it unchanged over
twenty-six electricity supplies in seven countries to find out where a
tariff-native demand controller's guarantees actually hold. Four results follow:
a per-step risk guarantee on a demand ceiling is under-protected roughly
sevenfold over a planning window while the textbook correction is $2.4\times$
too conservative; that risk level can be turned into an operator-set dial, at
measured and stated cost; distribution-free coverage degrades with aggregation
level; and forecast skill and available flexibility are independent axes, only
one of which tracks climate.

For India specifically the benchmark returns a divided verdict, and both halves
matter. India has the most favourable load physics in the study --- the highest
cooling share, the only strongly positive load--temperature relationship, a
peaky profile that a demand-charge tariff punishes and this method is built to
defend. It also returns the lowest forecast skill and the worst calibration we
measured, and there exists no Indian building-level dataset on which to close
the loop properly. The first half is the case for the work. The second half is
the work that remains, and Section~\ref{sec:india-fix} states it as such rather
than as a footnote.

Every number is regenerated by one script from a clean checkout, and the results
that contradict us are printed at the same size as the rest.
