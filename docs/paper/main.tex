\title{\bf What a Chance Constraint Does Not Promise\\[2pt]
\large Measuring a demand-ceiling guarantee across thirty-seven electricity supplies\\
in eight countries, with India as the focal case}

\author{%
  Swetank Kumar\\
  \small Project Samanvay / Aethergrid
}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Indian commercial demand charges are set by a single worst thirty-minute block
in a month, which makes defending a demand ceiling a risk problem rather than a
prediction problem. The standard applied response is to substitute a high
forecast quantile into the capacity constraint and call the result a
$95\%$ guarantee. We measure what that guarantee is actually worth, on
fourteen held-out windows across an American office and an Indian campus at
native 15-minute resolution. The per-step exceedance rate is $0.03$--$0.08$
against a nominal $0.05$ on every window --- marginally, the forecaster does
what it promised --- while the probability of breaching the ceiling
\emph{somewhere} in the controller's 16-hour planning window is $0.39$ to
$0.82$, median $0.57$: eight to sixteen times the level the constraint appears
to promise, and not a constant, since one meter returns $0.39$, $0.64$ and
$0.42$ in three successive Junes while its per-step rate does not move. The gap
narrows but persists --- median nine times, lowest day-block bootstrap bound
three times --- when the per-step layer's adaptive step is restated in units
that transfer across meter sizes.
Neither textbook substitute recovers it: the independence calculation
$1-(1-\alpha)^H$ overstates the risk by $1.7\times$ at the median because
load errors are strongly autocorrelated, and the Boole/Bonferroni union bound
--- the only one of the two that is a valid bound --- is $1$ on every window.
A Student-$t$ copula over the horizon predicts the realised value to a mean
absolute error of $0.065$; we use it to restore the level as a settable
parameter with a scenario MILP, tracking requested $\varepsilon$ at rank
correlation $0.957$ for \rupee{3{,}339} on a billing month. On a second
control object --- an Indian campus feed under Delhi's own tariff --- the
response stays monotone (rank correlation $0.988$) and the exactness does not
survive, on the one series here whose per-step layer under-covers: a joint
guarantee is no more exact than the marginals it composes. We report its
exactness and resolution limits.

We test the rest of the pipeline by running one unchanged protocol over thirty
electricity supplies in eight countries: eighteen buildings across eight usage
classes from the Building Data Genome Project 2, six metered national or city
demand series including the Delhi State Load Despatch Centre and ENTSO-E, and
six Chinese provinces added late on an externally imposed constraint. Two
findings follow. Distribution-free coverage appeared to degrade with
aggregation level --- $0.901$ on buildings against $0.829$ on metered system
demand --- and does not: the adaptive layer's step is a number in the units of
the series, and at system scale it did not move. Under split conformal alone
the tiers are within $0.03$ of each other and eleven of eighteen buildings
miss $0.85$, so the exchangeability failure is universal; with the step stated
as a fraction of the interval width the same layer holds $0.90$--$0.93$ at
every tier and no supply in the study misses $0.85$. And forecast skill is
uncorrelated with climate while available flexibility tracks it strongly, so
deployment screening should ask how much load is weather-driven rather than how
predictable it is; the null replicates on the Chinese panel it was not fitted
to.

India is the focal case and returns a divided verdict: the most favourable load
physics in the study and the worst calibration in it. The China arm's most
quotable result --- that the closest structural analogue to Delhi returns the
second-worst skill in the benchmark --- is retracted in
Section~\ref{sec:china}, because auditing that dataset against its own
documentation showed a whole year of provincial demand to be $778$ numbers
reproducing $8{,}760$ values to the precision the source is published at, which
makes its skill column a statement
about the data rather than about a forecaster. Five rows argue against the
method, three of them outright negative, and all five are reported at the
same size as the rest.
\end{abstract}
