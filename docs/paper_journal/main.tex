\begin{abstract}
Indian commercial demand charges are set by the single worst thirty-minute
block in a month, which makes defending a demand ceiling a risk problem rather
than a prediction problem. The standard applied response substitutes a high
forecast quantile into the capacity constraint and calls the result a $95\%$
guarantee. This paper measures what that guarantee delivers, on fourteen
held-out windows across an American office and an Indian campus at native
15-minute resolution. Per step the bound behaves as advertised: realised
exceedance is $0.032$--$0.079$ against a nominal $0.05$ on every window. Over
the controller's 16-hour planning window the probability of breaching
\emph{somewhere} is $0.39$ to $0.82$, median $0.57$ --- eight to sixteen times
the level the constraint appears to promise --- and it is not a constant of the
formulation, since one meter returns $0.39$, $0.64$ and $0.42$ in three
successive Junes while its per-step rate does not move. Neither textbook
substitute recovers it: the independence calculation $1-(1-\alpha)^{H}$
overstates realised risk by $1.7\times$ at the median because load errors are
strongly autocorrelated, and the Boole union bound, the only one of the two
that is valid, is $1$ on every window. A Student-$t$ copula fitted on
validation data predicts the realised value to a mean absolute error of
$0.065$; using it, a scenario MILP restores the violation level as a settable
parameter, tracking the requested $\varepsilon$ at rank correlation $0.957$ for
\rupee{3{,}339} on a billing month, with its exactness and its resolution floor
reported. Repeated on a second control object in another country under another
tariff, the dial's monotonicity replicates (rank correlation $0.988$) and its
exactness does not, on the one series here whose per-step layer under-covers
--- a joint guarantee is no more exact than the marginals it composes. The same protocol run over thirty-seven electricity supplies in eight
countries yields two further results. Distribution-free coverage does
\emph{not} degrade with aggregation level, contrary to a reading this study
first made and then unmade by its own audit: the adaptive conformal layer's
step size is stated in the units of the series, and at system scale it had
stopped moving. And forecast skill and available flexibility are independent
deployment axes, only one of which tracks climate.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Nomenclature}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Nomenclature}
\begin{IEEEdescription}[\IEEEsetlabelwidth{$\hat q_{\alpha}(t)$}]
\item[$H$] Planning-horizon length, in 15-minute steps.
\item[$\alpha$] Nominal per-step exceedance level of the ceiling ($0.05$).
\item[$\hat\alpha$] Realised per-step exceedance on held-out data.
\item[$\hat q_{\alpha}(t)$] Calibrated forecast quantile at target time $t$.
\item[$\varepsilon$] Violation probability the scenario MILP is set to.
\item[$D_{\mathrm{peak}}$] Committed demand ceiling; a decision variable.
\item[$S$] Scenarios retained after reduction.
\item[$\gamma$] Adaptive-conformal step, in the units of the series.
\item[$\kappa$] The same step as a fraction of interval width.
\item[$\nu$] Degrees of freedom of the Student-$t$ copula.
\end{IEEEdescription}
