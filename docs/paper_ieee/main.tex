\begin{abstract}
Indian commercial demand charges are set by a single worst thirty-minute block
in a month, which makes defending a demand ceiling a risk problem rather than a
prediction problem. The standard applied response substitutes a high forecast
quantile into the capacity constraint and calls the result a $95\%$ guarantee.
We measure what that guarantee delivers, on fourteen held-out windows across an
American office and an Indian campus at native 15-minute resolution. Per step
the bound behaves as advertised: realised exceedance is $0.032$--$0.079$
against a nominal $0.05$ on every window. Over the controller's 16-hour
planning window the probability of breaching \emph{somewhere} is $0.39$ to
$0.82$, median $0.57$ --- eight to sixteen times the level the constraint
appears to promise --- and it is not a constant of the formulation, since one
meter returns $0.39$, $0.64$ and $0.42$ in three successive Junes while its
per-step rate does not move. Neither textbook substitute recovers it: the
independence calculation $1-(1-\alpha)^{H}$ overstates realised risk by
$1.7\times$ at the median because load errors are strongly autocorrelated, and
the Boole union bound, the only one of the two that is valid, is $1$ on every
window. A Student-$t$ copula fitted on validation data predicts the realised
value to a mean absolute error of $0.065$ and lies inside the day-block
bootstrap interval on twelve of fourteen windows. Using it, a scenario MILP
restores the violation level as a settable parameter: realised violation of the
ceiling the optimiser committed to tracks the requested $\varepsilon$ at rank
correlation $0.957$, for \rupee{3{,}339} on a billing month, with a resolution
floor of one scenario's probability mass.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A commercial consumer in India pays for energy and, separately, for the single
highest demand block recorded in the billing month. Defending a ceiling on that
block is the object of a large applied literature, and the standard
formulation is a per-interval chance constraint discharged by substituting a
high forecast quantile,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:marginal}
\Pr\!\left[\,y_t \le \hat q_{0.95}(t)\,\right] \ge 0.95
\quad \text{for each } t \text{ in the window,}
\end{equation}
after which the controller is described as offering a $95\%$ guarantee. The
event an operator cares about is not \eqref{eq:marginal}. It is that no block
in the planning window exceeds the ceiling, and that is a joint event over $H$
correlated steps. The two statements differ, and this paper measures by how
much on metered load.

The measurement is worth making because both of the usual corrections fail, and
they fail in opposite directions. The quantity most often quoted,
$1-(1-\alpha)^{H}$, is the independence calculation and not a bound; load
errors are strongly autocorrelated, so it overstates the risk. The
Boole/Bonferroni union bound is valid and, at $\hat\alpha\approx0.05$ over a
sixteen-hour horizon, vacuous. Marginal calibration therefore pins the
horizon-level risk only to a wide interval, and nothing about the forecaster
tells an operator where in that interval a deployment sits.

\paragraph*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A measured bracket on horizon-level risk for a demand ceiling on held-out
      meter data, over fourteen windows in two countries at native 15-minute
      resolution, with day-block bootstrap intervals: claimed $0.05$, realised
      $0.39$--$0.82$, independence calculation $0.87$--$0.99$, union bound
      vacuous on every row (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
\item A closed-loop demonstration that this level can be made an operator-set
      parameter rather than an emergent property of horizon length, with its
      exactness, its resolution floor and its price measured
      (Section~\ref{sec:dial}).
\item The measurement is made on the first Indian building-level load published
      at native resolution for this purpose, under an Indian tariff
      (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Chance-constrained MPC for buildings was established by Oldewurtel
\emph{et al.}\cite{oldewurtel2012,oldewurtel2014}, which reformulates per-step
chance constraints under Gaussian disturbances; Zhang \emph{et
al.}\cite{zhang2013scenario} replaces the distributional assumption with
sampled scenarios in the sense of Calafiore and
Campi\cite{calafiore2006scenario}. Joint-in-time chance constraints are handled
in the general stochastic-MPC literature by risk allocation under Boole's
inequality\cite{ono2008risk,paulson2017joint}, which is conservative by
construction and, as Section~\ref{sec:gap} measures, vacuous at the horizon a
demand ceiling requires. A recent line combines conformal prediction with MPC
to obtain joint-in-time regions without a distributional
assumption\cite{lindemann2023safe,vogel2025conformalmpc}, evaluated on
trajectory-prediction and synthetic linear-system benchmarks; the one
application to building demand response we are aware
of\cite{xu2026e2edpc} uses stage-wise conformal tightening in simulation and
does not report horizon-level violation.

On demand-charge management specifically, the closest prior work is the PolyU
line on monthly peak limiting\cite{xu2019adaptive,xu2020exploration}:
probabilistic load forecasts feed a threshold-resetting scheme, with Monte
Carlo used to study robustness of the \emph{threshold} rather than to bound the
probability of exceeding it. Conformal methods for multi-step time series
provide the marginal layer we
use\cite{romano2019cqr,gibbs2021adaptive,stankeviciute2021conformal,sun2024copula}.
What is missing across these threads, and what this paper supplies, is a
measurement on metered building load of the horizon-level breach probability a
marginally calibrated constraint actually delivers, bracketed by the two
substitutes, and its restoration as a settable level inside a demand-charge
MILP.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph*{Data} Two corpora, one protocol. The Building Data Genome Project
2\cite{miller2020bdg2} supplies an all-electric office in Phoenix
(13{,}759\,m$^2$). I-BLEND\cite{rashid2019iblend} supplies seven buildings and
the campus transformer feed of IIIT-Delhi at one-minute native resolution,
blocked to fifteen minutes; it is, to our knowledge, the only open Indian
building-level load dataset with the coverage this protocol needs. Six of the
seven buildings are served by a central chiller that is not on their own meter,
so building rows are forecasting and calibration results only; the campus total
is the billed consumer and the control object.

\paragraph*{Protocol} Fifteen months train, two months validate, one month
test, temporal throughout, with conformal calibration fitted on the validation
block alone. The test month is June in every window. Quantile forecasts come
from gradient-boosted trees at five levels, calibrated by split conformal per
horizon and an adaptive layer\cite{gibbs2021adaptive} run in time order over
the evaluation window. Thirteen admissible (series, June) windows in Delhi plus
the Phoenix office give fourteen.

\paragraph*{Uncertainty} Coverage and exceedance are means over thousands of
forecast origins, but adjacent origins share 63 of their 64 steps, so the unit
of replication is the day rather than the origin. Every interval reported here
is a day-block bootstrap, resampling calendar days with replacement.

Table~\ref{tab:horizon-panel} reports, for each window, the per-step exceedance
rate of the ceiling and the probability that the ceiling is breached at least
once within a 16-hour window, alongside both substitutes.
Figure~\ref{fig:horizon} draws the same measurement against horizon length on
the Phoenix office.

Three readings are simultaneously true. First, \emph{the forecaster is not the
problem}: $\hat\alpha$ holds between $0.032$ and $0.079$ against a nominal
$0.05$ on every window, which is the calibration layer doing its job. Second,
\emph{the formulation is under-protected by roughly an order of magnitude}:
realised horizon exceedance runs $0.393$ to $0.818$, median $0.565$, and no
window's bootstrap lower bound falls under $0.28$. A controller deployed on
\eqref{eq:marginal} and described as a $95\%$ guarantee is running at roughly
$60\%$ on the mildest window in the table and below $20\%$ on the worst. Third,
\emph{neither substitute recovers it}. The independence figure sits between
$0.87$ and $0.99$, overstating realised risk by $1.66\times$ at the median. The
Boole bound is $1$ on every row: at $\hat\alpha\approx0.05$ it reaches unity by
$H=17$, four hours into a sixteen-hour horizon, after which it says nothing.

The gap is also not a constant that could simply be published as a correction.
The Academic building returns $0.393$, $0.636$ and $0.422$ in three successive
Junes while its per-step rate stays within $0.002$ of $0.05$, and 32 of the 91
pairs of bootstrap intervals in the table are disjoint. The correction is
itself a random variable with a wide law.

\subsection{Robustness to the Calibration Layer's Own Tuning}

The per-step column is produced by an adaptive layer whose step size is a
number in the units of each series, and these meters range from intervals of a
few kilowatts to several hundred. Restating that step as a fraction of the
interval width and rerunning all fourteen windows leaves the finding in place
and changes its size: realised exceedance then runs $0.254$ to $0.660$, median
$0.440$, with the lowest bootstrap bound at $0.15$. Under either calibration,
on every window, the marginal constraint delivers between three and sixteen
times its nominal horizon risk. Which end of that range a deployment lands on
depends in part on a tuning choice nobody making it would expect to be choosing
a risk level.

\section{Restoring the Level as a Dial}
\label{sec:dial}

\input{tables/acceptance}

We fit a Student-$t$ copula over the horizon on the validation block and sample
joint trajectories --- the marginals-to-scenarios construction of Pinson
\emph{et al.}\cite{pinson2009scenarios} with a heavier-tailed dependence family
--- which a scenario MILP then constrains with a CVaR
surrogate\cite{rockafellar2000cvar,nemirovski2006convex} on the probability
mass permitted to violate. The copula reproduces the calibrated marginals to
within $4.6\%$ of mean interval width at $4{,}000$ paths per origin, so it adds
dependence and disturbs nothing upstream, and it predicts horizon exceedance
far closer than either substitute: across the fourteen windows its mean
absolute error is $0.065$ against $0.382$ for the independence figure, and it
lies inside the realised value's $95\%$ interval on twelve of fourteen, where
the independence figure lies inside none.

Its one free parameter, the degrees of freedom, is selected on the validation
block by matching predicted to realised horizon exceedance --- the quantity it
is later judged on. Holding that parameter at the value chosen on the Phoenix
office and applying it unchanged to all thirteen Indian windows still lands
inside the realised interval on nine of thirteen, so the selection buys
accuracy rather than the agreement itself. Selecting it instead by
pseudo-likelihood on the same block picks the near-Gaussian end of the grid on
every window and over-predicts on all fourteen: the likelihood is dominated by
the body of the distribution, where the families agree, while the horizon event
lives in the joint upper tail.

Table~\ref{tab:acceptance} is a closed-loop run over a real billing month at
two demand targets and four risk levels. The acceptance metric is violation of
the ceiling \emph{the optimiser itself committed to at each origin}, because
that is the event $\varepsilon$ is a statement about; the operator's monthly
target is a business metric the chance constraint never promised, since the
committed peak is a decision variable and an optimiser short of capacity may
lift it, pay the demand charge and honour its constraint exactly. Both columns
are shown and only one is the criterion.

The result is that $\varepsilon$ becomes a dial. Realised commit violation
tracks the requested level at rank correlation $0.957$ across the sweep, mean
absolute gap $0.057$, conservative at five of eight levels. It is not exact: at
the tightest setting $\varepsilon=0.05$ delivers $0.097$ at the tight target,
on the unsafe side. There is a hard resolution floor at $1/S$: with $S$
scenarios the finest expressible level is one scenario's worth of probability
mass, below which the CVaR surrogate collapses onto the sample maximum. That is
a property of sample average approximation, not a tuning artefact, and a finer
dial costs scenarios and solve time.

\section{Limitations}

The closed-loop result is one building over one month; it is the deepest
measurement here and the narrowest. Base-load and PV scenarios are drawn
independently, while cloud raises cooling load at the same moment it cuts PV
output, so the true joint law has worse afternoons than these scenarios
contain. The horizon is sixteen hours while the demand charge bills a monthly
maximum over roughly $2{,}880$ intervals: a joint guarantee over the window is
strictly stronger than a marginal one and strictly weaker than a monthly one,
and extending it needs the copula fitted over a month-length horizon, which is
a $2{,}880$-square correlation matrix and needs structure rather than a raw
estimate. Weather at the target time is taken as a perfect forecast, which
flatters the forecaster uniformly across the comparison.

\section{Conclusion}

A per-step chance constraint on a demand ceiling, marginally calibrated and
behaving exactly as specified, is under-protected over a sixteen-hour planning
window by a factor of eight on the building where it was first measured, and by
between three and sixteen across fourteen windows in two countries. The figure
usually quoted as the conservative correction is not a bound and overstates the
risk; the correction that is a bound is vacuous four hours in. The practical
consequence runs in both directions --- a controller sized off the independence
figure is over-protected by more than a factor of two and pays for it in the
usable headroom the whole economic argument rests on. Restoring the level as a
parameter costs \rupee{3{,}339} on a billing month and makes a quantity that
was an emergent property of horizon length into one an operator sets.
